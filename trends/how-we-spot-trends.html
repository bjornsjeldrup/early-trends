How we spot trends early

Most viral products leave a trail long before they go mainstream. Our job is to find that trail while it’s still faint.

What we changed (Nov 2026). After reader feedback, we tightened our filter and relabelled late picks as Mainstream. Those pages stay at the same addresses, with the label and the dated first signal. Misses we write up live on the Case studies shelf.

What we watch

We track a broad mix of public signals: what people search for, what creators make videos about, what niche communities talk about, how fast stock moves at retailers, and how new launches are doing. No single signal tells the whole story. A trend only shows up when several of them start moving together.

A single loud week is not a trend. A paid burst of posts, a restock, or one creator’s good day can make a product jump. We treat that jump as a reason to look around, not as proof. What we trust is the same product getting harder to ignore in places that do not copy one another: people looking the name up on their own, a community mentioning it unprompted, separate creators, a retailer that starts to sell through, and a launch that keeps drawing attention after it should have gone quiet. Several of those rising together is the trail.

Independent matters as much as rising. If every mention traces back to one announcement, we are watching a campaign. If search, conversation, and the shelf each pick up on their own, we are watching demand. They have to point the same way after the first burst fades.

How we weigh them

Signals don’t count equally, and how much each one counts depends on the category. A beauty trend leaves a different trail than a gadget or a toy. We give the most weight to how fast interest is growing, not how big it already is. We weigh early signals higher than late ones, and genuine community interest higher than marketing. On top of that we apply our own scoring model and editorial judgement, refined pick by pick. That’s the part we keep to ourselves.

Size is a lagging fact. Growth that is speeding up matters more than size. A small product whose attention is accelerating is more interesting to us than a household name whose attention is merely large. A climb that gets steeper tells us more than a high plateau, or a climb that has been steady for a long time. One jump can be noise. A climb that keeps steepening, across more than one kind of signal, is what we write up. The pace is not the same in every aisle, and we do not publish a formula for how the pieces combine.

The mainstream filter

Before a product can become a pick, it has to pass a filter that weeds out things that have already peaked. If everyone already knows about it, it isn’t an early trend, however popular it is.

Already peaked is not the same thing as popular this week. A new color, a famous sighting, or a sale can put an old line back in the news. If that line has been a normal purchase for a long time, the news is a ripple on a finished wave. If a careful shopper would not be surprised this exists, it does not go out as an early pick. The page can stay, with an honest label.

When the clock starts

Every pick is dated from the first release of the whole product line, not from the day a clip spread, the day one shade sold out, or the day we noticed it. The clock starts when that line first shipped. A new color of an old line is not a new trend, a restock is not a birth date, and a viral week does not restart the product’s life. Dating from the hype would make old products look young every time they trended again.

The line is the unit, rather than the whole brand or one later variant. A parent brand can be old while a new line is genuinely new, and we date the new line. One item inside an older line can get a burst of attention, and we still date from the first release of that whole line. On the product page, read that first-release date before the story. The “identified on” date is only when we wrote the pick. A short gap can still be early. A long gap means we were late.

Early, Rising, and Mainstream

The label follows from that gap:

  • Early. The line is still young and the trail is faint. Most shoppers would not know the name. The label is a timing note, not a promise.
  • Rising. The climb is obvious. The product is no longer a secret, and it is not yet something you expect in every store.
  • Mainstream – not an early pick. We got there too late. The product can be excellent. The page stays at the same address, with this label and the line’s first release, so the lateness stays visible.

We look again after we publish. A pick can move from Early to Rising, or on into Mainstream, as the line ages or becomes common knowledge. We would rather change the label than keep calling a known product early.

Learning from misses

We review every pick at regular intervals afterwards and keep score of hits and misses. When we get it wrong, we write it up, and the model gets better each time. Longer stories of a whole craze live on the Case studies shelf.

A shelf that only shows wins teaches nothing. When a late pick has a genuinely earlier replacement in the same aisle, we move it to Our misses. The address does not change, and the page stays up as a product guide so you can see what moved and why. Case studies and Our misses do different jobs: one is a retrospective, the other is a miss we replaced.

The scorecard is public for the same reason. The holiday press note records how tracked picks actually landed: still early, still rising, or already mainstream when we called them. It is a record of the past, not a forecast and not a promise about the next page, so you can tell whether later batches are earlier than the first.

What you can do with a pick

Read the label before the enthusiasm. Look at an Early pick while it is still easy to miss, and do not rush because of the label. A Rising pick is for when you want the product and can accept that other people have found it. A Mainstream pick is a guide, not a discovery. Then read the first-release date on that product’s page, and believe the date if the line is older than the excitement. A shop link is a convenience, not a recommendation. Please do not treat a pick as an investment.

If you are choosing a present, the gift guides collect picks that are reasonable to give, and they keep these same labels. A mainstream item can be a good gift, and it is not described as early. To see the timing in practice, read a current early pick beside something on Our misses. New pages are free in any RSS reader, from the subscribe page. If a date or a label looks wrong, tell us.

What this is not

Signals are clues, not guarantees. Nothing here predicts future results. Many products show strong early signs and still fade.

What we won’t cover

Anything adult, drug- or vape-related, unsafe for kids, or built on hype schemes.
